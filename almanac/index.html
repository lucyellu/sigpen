A Field Guide Toward · Working Title

The Celestial
Almanac

A field guide to the species of stars, planets, and systems — organized not by what they are, but by where they are in their ripening.
not a grant proposal not a startup a project that wants a sketch file
Astronomy gives us databases. Field guides give us recognition. The Almanac is what sits between them — a way of looking at the sky that names kinds the way a birder names warblers, and tracks stages the way an orchardist tracks fruit.

§01 Premise

Every celestial object is somewhere on a curve. Stars are born, settle, age, and die. Planets accrete, differentiate, weather, and either ripen toward complexity or pass without ever becoming legible. The curve is real. The framework that names where each object sits on its curve is mostly missing.

The Almanac is a sketch of that framework — a typological field guide where each entry tells you not just what an object is, but what stage of itself it is, and what comes next.

The Earth Similarity Index ranks everything by how Earth-like it is. A peach scores low on banana-similarity. We need a better question.

§02 The Ripeness Curve

The shape repeats. The timescale does not. A G-dwarf orchard ripens on 10⁹-year scales; an M-dwarf system may ripen on 10¹⁰; a Hycean world, we don't yet know.

§03 Specimen Cards (sketches)

Each entry in the eventual almanac would read like this. Three stubs to start:

Type 01 · TERRESTRIAL
Mundus vitalis communis
"the common living world"
host: G/K dwarf
age: 1–8 Gyr
signal: O₂ + CH₄ disequilibrium
failures: runaway greenhouse, magnetic collapse
Earth. Possibly K2-18b. Our entire reference set is here, which is the problem.
Type 02 · POST-OCEANIC
Mundus aridus
"the dried world"
host: any
age: variable
signal: oxidized surface, thin atmosphere
failures: already failed (or transcended)
Mars. The world that didn't make it, or made it past and we missed the window.
Type 03 · OVERCOOKED
Mundus ardens
"the burning world"
host: any inner orbit
age: post-ripeness
signal: CO₂ choke, sulfuric clouds
failures: the obvious one
Venus. Possibly once-ripe. A cautionary entry; every almanac needs one.

§04 Layers the Almanac Would Track

Genealogy
Which stars share a birth cluster. Sibling systems. Stellar streams. The "litter" any given object came from.
Type
G-dwarf terrestrial, M-dwarf tightly-packed, Hycean, ocean world, tidally locked, post-oceanic. Each with its own ripening curve.
Stage
Where on its curve. Forming, settling, detectable, stressed, past. Same axis for everything, even though the units differ wildly.
Neighborhood
Where in the galaxy. GHZ position, local stellar density, recent flyby encounters, upcoming threats (red giant neighbors, supernova candidates).
Flux
Not just "what signals are present" but is the cycle closing? Are creation signals matched to decay signals? Fungi-equivalent processes running?
Biography
Each entry has a story, not just a row in a database. A field guide is read; a database is queried.

§05 What "Doing The Project" Looks Like

  • Pick 12 specimens. Real ones. Earth, Mars, Venus, Titan, Europa, Proxima b, TRAPPIST-1e, K2-18b, Kepler-452b, our Sun, Betelgeuse, the Pleiades cluster.
  • Draft a single-page entry for each in the specimen-card style above. Latin name (made up is fine), type, stage, signal markers, biography.
  • Build a simple web version. One page per specimen. Cross-links by type and neighborhood. Could be flat HTML; could be a small static site.
  • Steal aesthetics from real field guides: Sibley, Peterson, the Audubon plates. Hand-feel beats database-feel.
  • Resist the urge to be comprehensive. Twelve good entries is more useful than two hundred thin ones.
  • Share when ready. Watch what catches.

§06 Why It's Worth Doing (Even As Fun)

The night sky is one of the few things every human alive can look at directly. Modern astronomy has made it more known and less familiar — people see "stars" where they used to see kinds. A field guide that gave back the sense of distinct species of celestial things, each with its own life, would be a small act of relegibility.

Worst case: a nice weekend project that produces something beautiful.
Best case: a framework other people start adding entries to.
Either way: the sketch file stays open.
